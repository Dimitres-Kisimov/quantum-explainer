    House rules

    Every claim on this page is either demonstrated live on the simulator or backed by a reference [1–7] listed in the footer. If a sentence sounds like marketing, it doesn't belong here.

    1 · Qubits and superposition

    A classical bit is 0 or 1. A qubit's state is a pair of complex numbers — amplitudes — α and β with |α|² + |β|² = 1, written |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ [1, ch. 1]. Superposition is not "both values at once" in the everyday sense: it is one definite, known state that will give a random measurement result, with probabilities |α|² and |β|².

    The Hadamard gate H turns |0⟩ into (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2: both amplitudes equal 1/√2 ≈ 0.707, so each outcome has probability 0.5. A single qubit's state maps to a point on a sphere — the Bloch sphere — with |0⟩ at the north pole and |1⟩ at the south [1][3].

    Two qubits need 4 amplitudes; n qubits need 2ⁿ. That exponential bookkeeping is why ordinary computers struggle to simulate more than roughly 50 qubits — and it is the only place a quantum computer's advantage can come from.

    2 · Measurement

    Measuring asks the qubit "which basis state?" and gets one classical answer: outcome i with probability |amplitudei|² — the Born rule [1, ch. 2]. The state then updates to match the answer; the superposition is gone. You never see amplitudes directly. You infer them from statistics over many repeated runs, which is why the playground fires 1000 shots at a time.

    Honesty note about this app: the "randomness" here is a seeded pseudo-random generator so results are reproducible for teaching. Real hardware is genuinely random shot-to-shot — and noisy on top [2].

    3 · Interference — why amplitudes cancel

    Amplitudes are complex numbers, so they can subtract. Apply H twice to |0⟩. The two paths to the |1⟩ outcome arrive with amplitudes (1/√2)·(1/√2) = +1/2 and (1/√2)·(−1/√2) = −1/2. Sum: 0. The paths to |0⟩ both arrive at +1/2 and add to 1. So H·H|0⟩ = |0⟩ exactly — the randomness disappears.

    Interference is the actual working resource of quantum computing: an algorithm choreographs amplitudes so that paths to wrong answers cancel and paths to right answers reinforce [1]. It is also fragile — interaction with the environment (decoherence) scrambles the phases that make it work [2].

    Phases are invisible until they interfere: slip a Z between the two H's and the outcome flips to |1⟩ with certainty (H·Z·H = X).

    4 · Entanglement — the Bell state, step by step

    Start at |00⟩. H on q0 gives (|00⟩ + |10⟩)/√2. Now CNOT (control q0) flips q1 only in the branch where q0 is 1: the result is (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 — a Bell state [1, ch. 1][3].

    Check that it cannot be split into "q0's state" ⊗ "q1's state": every product state satisfies a₀₀·a₁₁ − a₀₁·a₁₀ = 0, but here the check gives (1/√2)(1/√2) − 0·0 = 0.5 ≠ 0. The playground prints this check after every two-qubit step.

    Measure it: you get 00 or 11, never 01 or 10 — individually random, perfectly correlated. Each qubit alone is maximally random: watch its Bloch arrow shrink to the centre of the sphere (a mixed state). The information lives in the correlation, not in either qubit.

    What entanglement does not do: it cannot send messages faster than light. Your partner's measurement results look like fair coin flips until you compare notes over an ordinary channel [1, ch. 2].

    5 · What quantum computers are NOT

    • Not faster at everything. Known speedups apply to specific problem families. For most computing — spreadsheets, video, databases, almost everything data centres do — there is no quantum speedup and classical machines remain the right tool. Quantum computers are not expected to solve NP-complete problems efficiently; for brute-force search the provable limit is Grover's quadratic speedup, no better [5].
    • Not "trying all answers in parallel". Putting a register into a superposition over all inputs is one H per qubit — the trivial part. A measurement then returns one outcome. Useful algorithms must arrange interference so wrong answers cancel, and only a few problem structures are known to allow that [1].
    • Not big or reliable yet. Today's devices are NISQ — Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum [2]: roughly hundreds to a few thousand physical qubits that decohere quickly, with error rates that limit circuits to modest depth. Fault tolerance requires quantum error correction, whose overhead is on the order of hundreds to thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit at realistic error rates [2][4].
    • Not breaking your encryption today. Running Shor's algorithm on RSA-2048 needs a large fault-tolerant machine; a widely cited resource estimate is ~20 million noisy physical qubits running for ~8 hours [4] — orders of magnitude beyond current hardware. The realistic concern is "harvest now, decrypt later", which is why post-quantum cryptography migration is happening now, ahead of any such machine.
    • Not a replacement for classical computers. Even the optimistic future is a quantum co-processor for a handful of special workloads, driven and error-corrected by classical machines.
    • Not settled in optimization/ML/finance. Claimed near-term speedups there are research, not established advantage, and often vanish against well-tuned classical baselines [2].

    Rule of thumb: if a headline says quantum computers will imminently make X obsolete, test it against this list.

    6 · Where they may genuinely help

    • Simulating quantum systems (molecules, materials): the original motivation and the most natural fit — a quantum system simulating another quantum system without the 2ⁿ blow-up [1][2].
    • Factoring and discrete logarithms: Shor's algorithm runs in polynomial time in the number of digits [6], versus sub-exponential for the best known classical method. This is why RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography are considered at risk once large fault-tolerant machines exist [4] — and why replacements are being standardized already.
    • Unstructured search: Grover's algorithm needs ~√N oracle queries where classical needs ~N [7] — a quadratic speedup, provably the best possible for this black-box setting [5]. Useful, but it does not turn hard problems into easy ones.

    Everything else currently sits between "interesting research direction" and "unknown". That is not a failure — it is what an honest snapshot of a young technology looks like [2].

    7 · What this simulator is (and isn't)

    This page stores the full state vector — 2 amplitudes for one qubit, 4 for two — and multiplies small matrices, exactly the arithmetic defined in the textbooks [1]. It is noiseless and tiny on purpose: 1–2 qubits is the regime where the mathematics can be shown completely and honestly. A phone can simulate ~20 qubits this way; nothing classical can go far beyond ~50. The teaching value is that what you see here is the real formalism, not an artist's impression.
